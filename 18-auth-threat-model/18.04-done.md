# Chapter 18 — Key Takeaways & Quiz

## Key Takeaways

Deliberately increasing the per-attempt cost of a verification operation imposes that cost on every attacker attempt while the legitimate user pays it once — an asymmetry worth exploiting wherever the legitimate latency is tolerable and the attacker volume is high.

Statelessness and revocability are in direct tension: a token that requires no server-side lookup cannot be cancelled by the server, and a token that can be cancelled always requires a server-side lookup. The two-token design does not resolve that tension — it splits the work so each token handles the job it is suited for.

A valid session token is not a permission to access any resource; it is proof of identity. Whether that identity is allowed to touch a specific row in a specific table is a separate question, enforced by a separate check — and skipping that check is how IDOR vulnerabilities appear in otherwise well-authenticated systems.

The appropriate signing scheme for a JWT is determined by who needs to verify the token: if verification happens inside one service that also issues the token, a shared secret is sufficient; if independent services need to verify tokens they did not issue, a published public key is the only approach that does not require distributing a secret.

Threat modelling is an exercise in choosing what not to defend against as much as what to defend against — a system that treats every possible attack as in scope produces controls that are expensive, complex, and often counterproductive for the actual risk profile of the application.

## Quick Quiz

**Q1.** An access token is stolen from a recruiter's browser. The attacker does not have the refresh token. What limits the damage — and what does not?

Think about: what the attacker can do for the next 15 minutes; what they cannot do after the token expires; whether the legitimate user's password matters in this scenario; and whether any server-side action can shorten the 15-minute window.

---

**Q2.** A colleague proposes storing refresh tokens as plaintext in the `refresh_tokens` table. "It's not a password — it's a random string, so there's nothing to brute-force." They are half right. Identify the specific attack scenario their proposal enables despite the "nothing to brute-force" argument, and explain why hashing with SHA-256 closes it. Then explain why bcrypt — the right choice for passwords — would be unnecessary for this case and what property of passwords makes bcrypt necessary there but not here.

---

**Q3.** Your system uses HS256 (symmetric) JWT signing. A frontend developer asks: "Can we let a third-party analytics service verify the JWT signature locally — on their own server, without calling our API — to confirm the user's role before displaying certain dashboard widgets?"

What is the problem with sharing what they would need to do that, and what signing scheme would let them verify tokens without creating that risk?
