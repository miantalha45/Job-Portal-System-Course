# The board is full of relationships you read across

Step back and look at what a job portal actually *does* all day. Almost every screen reads *across* related records:

- "Show this **company's jobs**, each with the company name and applicant count."
- "Show this **application** with its job title and the applicant's name and résumé."
- "Show the **public board** — jobs from every company, each labelled with its company."
- "Count each **recruiter's** new applications this week."

These are **joins**. A **join** combines related tables in a single query, matching rows by their keys — "for each job, find its company and bring back the name." Relational databases are *built* to do joins efficiently: you ask once, the database does the matching, you get one combined result.

Here's a real one your portal runs constantly — "this company's open jobs, with the company name":

```sql
SELECT j.title, j.location, c.name AS company_name
FROM jobs j
JOIN companies c ON c.id = j.company_id
WHERE j.company_id = $1
  AND j.status = 'open';
```

One query. The database matches each job to its company for you and hands back the combined rows.

## The trap the document model walks you into

Now picture the same screen in a document model, where jobs and companies sit in *separate* collections (a document store's word for tables). You can't join across them in one trip, so you fetch the jobs — say twenty of them — and then **loop**, fetching each job's company one at a time to get the name. That's **1 query for the jobs + 20 more for the companies = 21 queries** to render one page. Make the board bigger and it gets worse linearly.

That pattern has a name — the **N+1 query problem**: one query to get a list of N things, then N more queries (one per item) to fill in their related data. It's one of the most common performance killers in real systems, and a document model that scatters related data across collections nudges you straight into it. You'll meet N+1 head-on, and learn the fixes, in [Chapter 40](../40-n-plus-1/) — but the cheapest fix is to start on a foundation where the *single-query* join is the natural thing to write, not the clever thing you retrofit.

> **A quick word on schema, since it's the thing you're tempted to give up.** A defined **schema** feels like a constraint when you're moving fast — but it's exactly what lets the database *understand* your data well enough to optimise it: to index it, to plan an efficient join, to reject a malformed write before it rots your data. The "rigidity" you'd trade away is what buys you query power and safety — and a job portal cashes those in on nearly every screen.
